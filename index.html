A thought experiment for Chris Miller

You did the research. What if it never became a book?

Chris Miller spent years mapping the silicon supply chain and turned it into Chip War — 400 printed pages, a static object, frozen the day it shipped. But the research was a model of a living system. A book is only one render of it.

Below are twelve working prototypes of the same research told as something other than a nonfiction book — a scroll you fall down, a map you can break, a war you can play, a book you can argue with, a play you can stage. Each is a real, clickable demo. The last one is a dial that fast-forwards the media tech 18 months — because if you'd spent that year chasing robots instead, the tools to tell this story would have moved too.

01 — SCROLLYTELLING

Silicon Scroll

You don't read the exponential. You fall down it.

A printed book flattens an exponential into a static log chart and trusts you to imagine the motion. Here the curve actually outruns your scroll, the cost bars blow past the top of the screen, and the map of the world's fabs goes dark until one dot is left blinking.

What the book can't do: make you physically feel a 58-million-fold increase, or watch leading-edge manufacturing collapse onto a single island in real time.

Act I · Moore's Law

2,300 → 208,000,000,000

The Intel 4004 (1971) held 2,300 transistors. Nvidia's 2024 Blackwell B200 holds 208 billion across two dies — and Apple's M2 Ultra packs 134 billion. Roughly a doubling every two years, for fifty years. Scroll, and watch the line claw up a log scale.

Act I · the trick of the log

The chart lies to calm you

That smooth line is drawn on a logarithmic axis. Flip it to linear and 50 years of progress becomes a flat floor with a wall at the end. The log scale is a choice — it makes the unimaginable look like a tidy diagonal.

Act II · the cost of staying on the curve

The bill comes due

Keeping pace got brutally expensive. A 28nm fab cost roughly $6B. A 2nm-class fab runs ~$20-28B — and a single EUV scanner inside it costs ~$200M ($380M for High-NA). Scroll and watch the 2nm bar grow past the top of the frame.

Act II · the survivors

From a crowd to a duopoly

The line threading the bars counts how many companies can still afford to build at each node. Around a dozen could do 28nm. Today only three — TSMC, Samsung, Intel — can mass-produce sub-7nm. At 2nm, really two.

Act III · one island

Where the advanced chips actually get made

Here is the world's chip output, scattered by region. Now filter to the leading edge — 5nm and below — and watch every dot fade except a cluster off the coast of China.

Act III · the silicon shield

~90% — on one island

Roughly 90% of the world's most advanced logic is fabricated in Taiwan. That single blinking dot is the most concentrated point of failure in the modern economy. The book argues this in a paragraph. The scroll shows you the lights going out.

02 — EXPLORABLE MAP

Chokepoint

Click a node. See who holds the knife.

The book tells the supply chain as a 400-page narrative. This renders it as a graph you wander: sand → wafer → ASML's EUV scanner → Japanese photoresist → TSMC → Nvidia → the device in your pocket. Click any node for the dossier — and hit "Cut this node" to watch the cascade propagate downstream.

What the book can't do: let you sever a link with your own cursor and feel "the supply chain is fragile" turn from a claim into a system you just broke.

03 — PLAYABLE SIMULATION

Win the Chip War

Six turns. Three chokepoints. One supply chain you can't unsnarl.

The book tells you export controls "backfired." This lets you impose them — and watch SMIC stockpile, reverse-engineer, and indigenize while your own firms lose the China market. Pick a side, pull three levers a year for six years, and learn the feedback loops by failing inside them.

What the book can't do: make the tradeoff between near-term denial and long-term self-sufficiency show up in your own score.

By end-2027 the random-event table is replaced by a reasoning adversary: an on-page model role-plays the "PRC planner" or "US BIS official," reacts to your exact moves, and trash-talks in character — no two runs alike. Negotiate the Dutch into multilateral controls out loud with a voice model, and the whole sim, opponent included, runs offline on the static page with no API key.

04 — CONVERSATIONAL

Interrogate the Book

Stop reading the index. Start cross-examining the argument.

Type a real question — "Why couldn't China just buy ASML's machines?" — and the book answers in its own voice, cites the chapter, and offers two ways to go deeper. Every claim becomes a launchpad to the next question.

What the book can't do: notice you misunderstood the EUV chokepoint and re-explain it three ways, or follow up when you ask "but what about Japan?"

05 — STEERABLE AUDIO

SteerCast

A podcast that shuts up and answers your question.

An audiobook is one fixed path, one fixed depth, for every listener. SteerCast is a branching conversation between two synthetic hosts — an explainer and a skeptic — that you steer in real time. Ride the spine, or interrupt to go three levels deep on EUV. It actually talks (your browser's speech engine), and it builds the personalized chapter you heard.

What the book can't do: let a curious listener dig while a casual one takes the express route — and update its own script when the news moves.

⏵ Tech in 18 months

Today it fakes the hosts with hand-authored branches and robotic browser TTS. By end-2027, cheap streaming voice models running locally make the two hosts sound like real ChinaTalk co-hosts with natural barge-in — you talk over them and they pivot. A small on-device LLM generates branches live from a vetted source pack, so any question ("CXMT's HBM situation this week?") gets a grounded, freshly-spoken answer.

06 — LIVING DOCUMENT

The Living Chip War

Chris Miller wrote the book. The agents keep writing the next page.

The narrative spine stays fixed, but every claim with a shelf life — node names, fab timelines, Entity List counts, market shares — is a live cell bound to a watched source. A fleet of agents reads the wire daily, drafts diffs, checks them against primary documents, and surfaces them in a "What Changed" feed. Read the book as Miller closed it in 2022 — or as it stands today.

What the book can't do: tell you which of its sentences went stale, by how much, and show you the receipt.

The human approval gate becomes the exception: agents poll the Federal Register, SEC EDGAR 6-Ks, and earnings transcripts in near-real-time, and verified diffs publish autonomously while only narrative-altering edits route to a human. "Second edition" stops being a multi-year event and becomes a daily background process.

What the book can't do: make you feel an exponential in your chest, or hear a supply chain collapse to a single sustained note.

09 — THEATER

The Silicon Stage

A docudrama in three fabs. The people, not the parts.

Strip the charts away and the chip war is a series of rooms where a few people made outsized bets: the "traitorous eight" walking out on Shockley, Morris Chang describing a foundry to a baffled minister, a US envoy leaning on the one country that makes the one machine. This is the book as a stage play — pick a scene, press play, and watch the spotlight follow whoever's speaking.

What the book can't do: give these moments a body and a silence. The most loaded line in the chip war is a pause an actor holds.

10 — COUNTERFACTUAL

The What-If Engine

History was contingent. Pull a thread and watch it unravel.

The book's quiet argument is that the chip world we got was never inevitable — a few sales, bets, and bans could have gone the other way. This makes that argument operable: choose a fork in the road and read the alternate present it produces, with a historians'-plausibility meter on how real the counterfactual is.

What the book can't do: let you A/B test history — hold a moment, change one variable, and watch the whole map redraw.

So — what could you have done?

The book was never the only render.

A nonfiction book is a brilliant compression format: portable, linear, authoritative, frozen. But the research underneath Chip War was a model of a living, adversarial system — exponentials, chokepoints, feedback loops, a clock. Those are exactly the things a printed page is worst at and an interactive artifact is best at. The twelve prototypes above aren't replacements for the prose. They're what the prose could become when you stop assuming "book" is the last step.

And the robots?

You asked me to price in 18 months of progress — what if that year went into robotics instead. Here's the bet baked into the Projector: by end-2027 the same author who mapped silicon is writing about embodied AI, because the next chokepoints are physical. China controls ~90% of the rare-earth magnets that move actuators (the Dy/Tb that turn an F-35 control surface, an Optimus arm, a Virginia-class sub). Harmonic-drive actuators are a Japanese near-monopoly. World-model weights are the new EDA; Nvidia compute is the new TSMC. Robot War isn't a metaphor — it's the same supply-chain logic walking off the wafer and into a body.

And the medium moves with it. The faked demos here — CSS-animated EUV beams, browser TTS, a stick-figure tutor — are end-2026 placeholders for what genuinely ships by end-2027: real-time generative video, on-device voice, agentic living docs, VLA-driven robot tutors. Spend the year on robots and you don't just get a new subject. You get a new set of tools to tell it with.
